How to Set Your Freelance Hourly Rate (A Simple Formula)

5 November 2026 · 3 min read

Most new freelancers set their rate by looking at what an employee in the same job earns per hour and adding a little. That almost always leaves them underpaid. An employee's hourly wage sits on top of paid holidays, sick pay, a pension contribution, equipment and an employer who finds the work. As a freelancer you pay for all of that yourself.

Here's a formula that takes those costs into account. You can run your own numbers in the free freelance hourly rate calculator as you read.

Step 1: Decide what you want to take home

Start with the yearly income you'd like after business costs but before income tax. If you're leaving a job, your old gross salary is a sensible starting point. Add roughly 20–30% if your job came with a pension match, health insurance or other benefits you'll now pay for yourself.

Step 2: Add your business costs

List everything the business pays for in a year: software subscriptions, a laptop spread over three years, accounting, insurance, a co-working desk, phone, website, training. Most solo freelancers land somewhere between 5% and 15% of revenue. Add the total to your target income.

Step 3: Count your real billable hours

This is the step people skip. There are about 2,080 working hours in a year, but you won't bill anywhere near that many.

ItemHours
Working weeks (52 minus 4 weeks holiday, 1 week sick, 1 week public holidays)46 weeks
Hours available (46 × 40)1,840
Admin, sales, invoicing and quiet periods (about 35%)−644
Billable hoursabout 1,200

Between 1,000 and 1,400 billable hours a year is typical for a full-time freelancer. If you're just starting and don't have a steady flow of clients yet, plan on the low end.

Step 4: Divide, then add a profit margin

Hourly rate = (target income + business costs) ÷ billable hours. Then add 10–20% as a buffer for late payers, quiet months and the clients who always want "one more small change".

Example: a target of 60,000 plus 6,000 of costs, over 1,200 billable hours, gives 55 per hour. With a 15% buffer that's about 63. An employee on 60,000 earns about 29 per hour, so the freelance rate is a little over double. That's normal.

Step 5: Sanity-check against the market

Look at what others in your field and location charge on job boards, freelancer directories and in communities you belong to. If your number is far above the market, you either need a niche that justifies it or to lower your costs. If it's far below, raise it: clients often read a low rate as low quality.

Day rates and project prices

Once you know your hourly rate, a day rate is usually 7–8 times it, because a "day" includes breaks and context-switching. For fixed-price projects, estimate the hours, multiply by your rate, then add 20–30% for unknowns. The project price calculator does this for you.

Don't forget tax

Your rate is before income tax. Put a percentage of every payment aside the day it arrives. Our guide on how much to set aside for taxes explains how to choose that percentage.

Make invoicing painless

Once you've set the rate, get paid quickly. Use the free invoice generator for one-off invoices, or an app like FreshBooks or Bonsai if you want reminders, payment links and expense tracking in one place.
